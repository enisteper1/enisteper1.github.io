After field weighting, should we tune BM25?

November 2026 · 4 min read

In the field-weighting experiment, giving product names more influence helped WANDS, while giving passage text more influence barely helped HotpotQA. But both experiments kept BM25’s parameters at their defaults. What happens if we keep those field weights and tune BM25 itself?

The biggest gain now comes from HotpotQA: another 3.43 nDCG points, with better recall too. Quora and WANDS improve only slightly. The dataset that benefited least from field weighting had the most to gain from parameter tuning.

Two knobs, different jobs

Field weights decide how much each part of a document contributes. BM25’s parameters decide how matches inside each field are scored.

k1: how much should repetition help? A passage mentioning “bridge” five times may be more relevant than one mentioning it once, but five mentions should not automatically earn five times the score. BM25 gives diminishing returns to repetition. A lower k1 makes those returns shrink sooner; a higher value gives repeated matches more room to contribute. This changes scoring for each query term—it does not understand whether a passage answers the question.

b: how much should length matter? A long description has more opportunities to contain a query word than a short title. BM25 accounts for this by comparing a field’s length with the average length in that field’s index. With the same term count, increasing b lowers the contribution in an above-average-length field and raises it in a below-average-length one. At b=0, length has no effect; at b=1, the full length ratio is used. It is a relative adjustment, not a rule that shorter documents are always better.

The two parameters interact: length normalization affects the term-frequency calculation that k1 controls. Lowering k1 can therefore reduce sensitivity to length differences as well as repetition. Keeping b fixed does not isolate a pure repetition effect.

I tested nine pairs: k1 ∈ {0.6, 1.2, 2.0} and b ∈ {0, 0.75, 1}. The baseline is k1=1.2, b=0.75. Each field still has its own BM25 index, with the same parameter pair applied to every field. Tokenization and field weights stay fixed.

Quora and HotpotQA parameters are selected by dev nDCG@10 and then frozen for test. WANDS uses five seeds of two-way two-fold cross-validation for parameter selection. Its field weights, however, were already chosen using these same queries in the previous experiment. That makes WANDS a conditional follow-up, not an independent test of the whole tuning process. No confidence intervals are reported here.

HotpotQA: a lower k1 does the work

Dev selection chose k1=0.6, b=0.75: keep length normalization as it was, but saturate term frequency sooner. On test, nDCG@10 rises from 0.5980 to 0.6323. MRR@10 rises from 0.7657 to 0.8049, and Recall@100 from 0.7583 to 0.7772. The gain is not bought by losing candidate coverage.

This changes the story from the previous post. Concatenated title and text scored 0.5950 nDCG and 0.7739 Recall@100. Field weighting alone nudged nDCG up but reduced recall. Adding parameter tuning now beats that original baseline on both: 0.6323 and 0.7772.

Why might this help? A passage can repeat an entity’s name many times without supplying the fact the question asks for. Saturating sooner could stop that repetition from outweighing matches to other query terms. The weaker sensitivity to length could also help longer relevant passages compete. These are plausible explanations, not findings from a query-level analysis: the measured result is that lowering k1 helped this weighted system.

The experiment does not test whether parameter tuning without field weighting would do just as well. It shows the extra gain from changing the scoring behavior after the field weights are fixed.

Quora and WANDS: smaller gains

Quora selected k1=0.6, b=1, but test nDCG improved by only 0.13 points. The clearest dev result was what to avoid: removing length normalization. At k1=1.2, changing b from 0.75 to 0 dropped dev nDCG from 0.7968 to 0.6554. Short questions still benefit from accounting for length.

A possible reason is that a short question matching the key words is often more focused than a longer question that merely includes them. Length normalization can favor that concentrated match. Repeated words also have limited room to matter in short questions, which may help explain why tuning beyond the default adds little. The scores support keeping normalization; they do not establish this explanation for individual queries.

On WANDS, the best full-query grid setting was k1=0.6, b=0.75, scoring 0.7301 nDCG. That is exploratory; choosing parameters on one half and measuring on the other gives 0.7269, only 0.29 points above the fixed-weight baseline. MRR barely moves, from 0.7031 to 0.7033, while Recall@100 gains 0.40 points.

WANDS may have less left to gain because field weighting already reduces the influence of long descriptions and attribute lists while favoring short product names. Changing saturation can still help, but it cannot tell whether a matched word is the requested product type or just a modifier—the failure mode from the previous post. This is a plausible reason for the smaller gain, rather than evidence that product search never benefits from parameter tuning.

The WANDS baseline here is 0.7239, not the previous post’s 0.7188. This experiment freezes the most frequent weight vector; the previous OOF system used different weights in different folds. Their gains should not be added together as though they came from one evaluation.

The takeaway

Field weighting was the bigger win on WANDS; parameter tuning was the bigger win on HotpotQA. Halving k1 added 3.43 nDCG points on top of HotpotQA’s weighted fields and recovered the recall lost in the previous experiment. Quora and WANDS gained much less.

Choosing field weights does not finish the job. A small parameter sweep can reveal that the default scoring behavior is holding retrieval back—but the useful setting and the size of the gain still depend on the dataset.
