Which tokenizer should BM25 use? 17 options on three datasets

November 2026 · 8 min read

BM25 is only as good as the terms it counts. We indexed three datasets (Quora duplicate questions, HotpotQA passages and WANDS furniture products) 17 times each with the same BM25 (k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75) and changed only the tokenizer: word tokenizers, stemming, pretrained subword vocabularies, character n-grams and word bigrams. Scores are averages over every evaluated query.

The basics

What is tokenization?

Turning a string into the list of terms a search engine will index and look up. A BM25 index maps each term to the documents that contain it, and a query term matches only if its string is exactly equal to an indexed term. Tokenization decides what those strings are.

Why does it matter for search?

One character can break a match. If a document is indexed with programming? and the query contains programming, the engine sees two unrelated terms. The same tokenizer must also run on both documents and queries, otherwise the two sides never meet.

How is it different from stemming and lemmatization?

Tokenization splits the text. Stemming and lemmatization then normalize each token so that word forms match. A stemmer cuts suffixes with fixed rules: fast and crude, and the result is not always a real word. A lemmatizer looks words up in a dictionary and returns the base form, but it needs the part of speech to do it well. Lowercasing is the simplest normalization and every tokenizer here does it.

What about subwords and n-grams?

Subword tokenizers come from neural language models. They keep common words whole and split rare words into pieces from a fixed vocabulary. We used the vocabularies of BERT (WordPiece), RoBERTa (BPE) and T5 (Unigram) as plain tokenizers, without loading any model. Character n-grams cut every word into overlapping slices of n letters. Word bigrams index pairs of neighbouring words as extra terms. The two hybrids add bigrams or character 3-grams to the regex words in the same index.

The results

Same 17 tokenizers, same BM25, three very different corpora. Pick a dataset to see its table and the change in nDCG@10 against whitespace splitting, or open Compare for all three side by side.

Punctuation is the cheapest win

Splitting on whitespace leaves programming? as one token, so it never matches programming. A one-line \w+ regex fixes that: +6.7 nDCG points on Quora and +8.5 on HotpotQA, in about the same time as whitespace splitting. On WANDS it is worth only +0.6. Queries there are short product phrases like salon chair, and the example product description in the notebook is already spaced around punctuation, so there is little left to fix.

Smarter word tokenizers did not help

NLTK Treebank, NLTK Tweet and spaCy never beat the regex: they stayed within a point of it on Quora and WANDS (−0.9 to +0.1) and fell 2.6 to 3.3 points below it on HotpotQA. They keep ? and , as tokens, which carry no signal for BM25, and they split contractions into pieces. They were also 6 to 19 times slower than the regex.

Normalization mattered most

Stemming on top of the regex added 5.3 points on Quora, 2.4 on WANDS and only 0.4 on HotpotQA. Lemmatization added 3.3, 2.3 and 0.3. Quora is short duplicate questions where the same idea is phrased with different word forms, and WANDS shoppers type tables against descriptions that say table, so conflation pays. HotpotQA questions are built around names and Wikipedia entities, which stemming barely touches.

On HotpotQA and WANDS the lemmatizer is within 0.1 points of Porter in about a third of the time (284 s against 864 s, and 3.6 s against 13.3 s). Only on Quora is Porter clearly ahead (+1.9 points). The lemmatizer had no part-of-speech tags, so it treated every word as a noun and left writing alone.

Character n-grams work like a fuzzy stemmer

Character 3-grams let programmers and programs share most of their terms, which is roughly what a stemmer does. On Quora that helped: 3-grams alone scored 2.1 points above the regex, and words plus 3-grams in one index came second overall, 0.5 behind Porter. The same hybrid was third on WANDS (+1.3 over the regex). Shorter and longer n-grams were worse: 2-grams match almost anything, and 5-grams are too long to bridge word forms.

On HotpotQA it went the other way. 3-grams alone fell 11.6 points below the regex and even 3.1 below whitespace, and the hybrid lost 3.1 to the regex. A three-letter slice is shared by a large share of 5.2 million passages, so it carries little information and pulls in many wrong ones. The hybrid is also expensive: documents become about four times longer, and indexing HotpotQA took 370 s instead of 65 s.

Pretrained subwords are not a free upgrade

The BERT, RoBERTa and T5 vocabularies helped a little on Quora (Unigram +1.4, WordPiece +0.7, BPE −0.1 against the regex) and lost everywhere else: WordPiece −3.2, BPE −6.5 and Unigram −7.8 on HotpotQA, and −2.2, −13.5 and −1.7 on WANDS. These vocabularies were built for neural models. They break rare words, such as names and product terms, into common pieces that match many unrelated documents. BPE also keeps the word-start marker, so the same word becomes two different terms depending on where it appears, which is the likely reason it was the worst of the three on WANDS. We did not test vocabularies trained on each corpus.

Word bigrams hurt in the same index

Adding word bigrams to the regex words lost 10.3 points on Quora, 1.9 on WANDS and 0.6 on HotpotQA. Bigrams alone were at or near the bottom everywhere. In one shared index a bigram is a rare term, so it gets a high IDF and a single phrase match can outweigh several word matches. The bigrams also double the document length, which changes BM25's length normalization, and the vocabulary grows from 86 thousand to 1.2 million terms on Quora. If phrases matter, a separate, lower-weighted field is the usual way to add them.

The takeaway

For BM25, how you normalize the text matters more than which tokenizer library you pick. A plain regex plus Porter stemming had the best nDCG@10 on all three datasets, +12.0 points over whitespace on Quora, +9.0 on HotpotQA and +2.9 on WANDS. Of the newer ideas only the words + character 3-gram hybrid came close, and only on the two smaller datasets. NLTK, spaCy and the pretrained subword vocabularies never reliably beat the regex, and word bigrams in the same index made things worse.
